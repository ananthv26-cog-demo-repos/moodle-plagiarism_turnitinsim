The Printing Press and the Making of Modern Europe (revised)
Priya Nair - ENG 201, Essay 1, second draft

In my first draft I leaned too hard on the encyclopedia, so this version is written in my own words. A printing press is, at its simplest, a machine that pushes paper against inked type hard enough and evenly enough to leave a clean copy. What made Gutenberg's version different from earlier stamps and woodblocks was the system around it: metal letters cast from a mould so they were interchangeable, an oil-based ink that stuck to metal, and a screw press adapted from wine making.

The point I want to make is about access. A hand-copied manuscript took a trained scribe months and cost as much as a small farm. A printed book could be sold for the price of a few days' wages. That price difference is the whole story. It is why the number of books in Europe went from thousands to millions within a single lifetime, and why people who had never expected to read began to.

Cheaper books also meant cheaper disagreement. Once a pamphlet could be set and printed overnight, an argument could spread through a city before the bishop had finished breakfast. Rulers and churches responded with censorship and licensing, which tells us how seriously they took the threat.

In conclusion, I now think of the press less as an invention and more as a price cut. Gutenberg did not invent reading, but he made it affordable, and an affordable idea is a dangerous one.
