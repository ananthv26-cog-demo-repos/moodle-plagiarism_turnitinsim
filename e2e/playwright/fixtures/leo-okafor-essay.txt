Information Before and After the Press
Leo Okafor - ENG 201, Essay 1

Historians often describe the arrival of printing as the beginning of mass communication. I agree with that framing, but I want to ask what "mass" actually meant to the people living through it. For most Europeans in 1450 information arrived by voice: a sermon, a town crier, a travelling merchant with news from the next valley.

The arrival of mechanical movable type printing in Europe in the Renaissance introduced the era of mass communication, which permanently altered the structure of society. The relatively unrestricted circulation of information and revolutionary ideas transcended borders, captured the masses in the Reformation, and threatened the power of political and religious authorities.

What strikes me is how quickly the authorities noticed. Licensing systems, lists of banned books, and printers' guilds all appear within a lifetime of Gutenberg's first Bible. A technology that lowers the cost of speaking is immediately followed by new attempts to control who gets to speak.

Across Europe, the increasing cultural self-awareness of its peoples led to the rise of proto-nationalism, and accelerated the development of European vernacular languages, to the detriment of Latin's status as lingua franca. I find that outcome the most surprising: a tool built to copy Latin scripture ended up making Latin less important.
